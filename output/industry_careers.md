**Assessment.** Industry is a small part of the post-35 divergence. (i) Sons of richer parents do sort into better-paid and steeper industries: shares in finance / insurance / real estate and legal / professional services rise with parental income (at 35-55: White top tercile 0.128 vs bottom 0.027; Black top tercile only 0.044), and the industry level carries 0.077 of the pooled wage gradient at 25-34 (the occupation score 0.16). (ii) But that industry component is in place by 25-34 and does not grow, and industry career slopes differ little (s.d. 0.037 log points per 10 years across men), so sorting into steep industries accounts for about 8% of the pooled rise of wage persistence after 35 and about 5% of the change of the Black-White gap (precisely estimated but small); level + slope about 8% of the divergence, not significant. (iii) Net of occupation, industry adds about 3% (n.s.) to the divergence and 6% to the pooled rise (all through slopes); with occupation and industry together, about 4/5 of the divergence remains within occupation x industry cells. (iv) The same-employer wage-growth gradient of White sons at 25-34 survives fine-industry fixed effects (0.021 -> 0.015); it is steepest inside finance / legal / professional / business services (0.040**) but is not confined to them, and Black sons show no gradient in any industry set. (v) Following the father: sons work in the father's industry group 2-3 times as often as random matching implies (White 0.142, Black 0.101 at 35-55); following in industry barely rises with parental income (following in occupation group does, for White sons). White sons whose fathers were in finance / insurance / real estate are 12 points more likely to be there; Black fathers were rarely in these industries (21 Black sons with a father in finance / insurance / real estate, 8 in legal / professional services), so Black industry-specific following cannot be estimated and the race difference is mainly in where fathers worked. Following carries a wage-level premium given education and the industry's pay (pooled 0.08*** at 25-34, 0.05* at 35-55) but no steeper wage growth or wage-tenure profile, and it accounts for essentially none of the divergence (0.002 of -0.107). A parental-network channel visible at the industry level therefore looks like an entry / level effect (in line with Staiger 2022 and Kramarz and Skans 2014 for employers), not career-stage growth. For the companion model this strengthens 27's reading: the divergence is differential human capital accumulation on the job (or its pay-off) by background and race within occupations, industries and employers; industry choice adds little. The NLSY79 shows the same small, flat industry components (its Black-White divergence is itself small and not significant). Caveats: descriptive; P and B are industry-average prices without holding worker composition fixed (education's steep slope partly reflects public pay scales), B is linear in age and fixed at 1992-2002; codes are noisy and bridged across classifications by modal / weighted crosswalks; the father's industry is his modal code at 12-18 among father-present sons (52% of Black, 87% of White sons, 24); employer transmission is not observable in the PSID.

Model: rel = f(age) + P_j + B_j x (age - 40)/10; P = wage level at 40 relative to all men, B = industry-specific age slope per 10 years relative to the average industry (both employment-weighted mean 0). ## 6. Literature on parental networks and following (verified on the source pages, 2026-10-17)

- Corak, M. and P. Piraino (2011), 'The Intergenerational Transmission of Employers', Journal of Labor Economics 29(1): 37-68 (Canada: about 40% of young men had at some point worked for an employer their father also worked for, 6-9% still had that employer as adults; transmission rises with paternal earnings, especially at the top). https://stonecenter.gc.cuny.edu/files/2011/08/corak-the-intergenerational-transmission-of-employers-2011.pdf ; IZA DP 4876 'About 6% of young Canadian men have the same main employer as their fathers': https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/izaizadps/dp4876.htm
- Kramarz, F. and O. Nordstrom Skans (2014), 'When Strong Ties are Strong: Networks and Youth Labour Market Entry', Review of Economic Studies 81(3): 1164-1200 (Sweden, linked employer-employee data: parents' workplaces are an important determinant of where graduates find their first job, more so for the low-educated and in high unemployment; larger when the parent's position is strong). https://www.ifau.se/en/Press/Abstracts/When-strong-ties-are-strong--networks-and-youth-labor-market-entry/
- Staiger, M. (2022), 'The Intergenerational Transmission of Employers and the Earnings of Young Workers', working paper, Opportunity Insights, December 2022 (US LEHD + 2000 Census: 29% work for a parent's employer at least once by age 30, 5% at the first job; working there raises initial earnings by 19%; children of higher-earning parents are more likely to do so and gain more; the elasticity of initial earnings with respect to parental earnings would be 7.2% lower without these jobs). https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f179912/f179912.pdf ; earlier version (2020: 7% of first stable jobs, 31% premium) https://equitablegrowth.org/working-papers/the-intergenerational-transmission-of-employers-and-the-earnings-of-young-workers/
- Chetty, R., M. O. Jackson, T. Kuchler, J. Stroebel, N. Hendren et al. (2022), 'Social capital I: measurement and associations with economic mobility', Nature 608: 108-121 (economic connectedness - the share of high-SES friends among low-SES people - is among the strongest predictors of upward mobility). https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v608y2022i7921d10.1038_s41586-022-04996-4.html
- Okumura, T. and E. Usui (2016), 'Intergenerational Transmission of Skills and Differences in Labor Market Outcomes for Blacks and Whites', Research in Labor Economics 43: 227-286 (NLSY79: White sons earn more in occupations requiring skills similar to their fathers', Black sons in such occupations earn less). https://ideas.repec.org/h/eme/rleczz/s0147-912120160000043015.html
- Laband, D. N. and B. F. Lentz (1992), 'Self-Recruitment in the Legal Profession', Journal of Labor Economics 10(2): 182-201 (lawyers' sons who receive transferred legal knowledge earn more). https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jlabec/v10y1992i2p182-201.html
- Chen, L., J. Gordanier and O. Ozturk (2016), 'Following (Not Quite) in Your Father's Footsteps: Task Followers and Labor Market Outcomes', MPRA Paper 76041 (Journal of Labor Research 2019) (task following carries a wage premium of about 5%). https://ideas.repec.org/p/pra/mprapa/76041.html

What is new here relative to these papers: they measure transmission of the employer itself at labour market entry (first stable job, initial earnings) in administrative data (Canada, Sweden, US LEHD) or the wage return to occupational / task following without the career profile. This file asks whether following the father's industry or occupation contributes to career-stage wage growth after 30 and to the Black-White divergence in wage persistence, in US survey data with parental income measured in childhood; it cannot see employers.
