## 1. Verdict in five lines

1. **Much improved; now a credible AEJ: Applied / JEEA submission as a facts-and-mechanism paper, still not top-5 material.** The headline fact is stated at its precision and survives every standard objection I raised (attrition, period, metric, reliability, three data sets). The mechanism section is one chain with a sharp schooling fact. The fathers' section is honest and, with the self-employment split, correct. The main text is 38 pages. That is the paper I asked for in round one, minus the model.
2. **The deal-breaker now is Section 7.** It is still a plan, and the revision has made the plan's weak point visible: its two race-specific mechanisms, access to good starting jobs and the pay-off of schooling in accumulation, each rest on one contrast at the margin of significance (race in access after education and the father's job 0.035\* (0.020); race in the education gradient of same-employer growth -0.007\*\* (0.003) in the PSID and -0.002 (0.002) in the NLSY79), and neither has been tested within the job cell, which is the only place where it is separable from sorting. A REStud/JPE referee will not accept counterfactuals built on two parameters whose separate identification is asserted and not shown.
3. **The second problem is one sentence in the abstract**: "rank-rank slopes do not diverge" is contradicted by the paper's own numbers (within-race ranks, both sexes: opening 0.093\*\* (0.043), Table 4; rank-slope gap widening 0.113\*\* (0.049) on the same children, Section 3.4). A Chetty-school referee will find it on page 1.
4. **Realistic journals.** As is: JOLE / JHR / EJ (acceptance likely), AEJ: Applied a real possibility (~35 percent). With the model estimated and the within-cell identification shown: AEJ: Applied or JEEA modal (~50 percent), REStud or JPE a genuine shot (~25 percent), AER/QJE ~10 percent (a PSID fact with public-data replication only; the PI's constraints rule out the administrative confirmation those journals would want, and the paper's falsifiable prediction for the 1978-83 IRS cohorts is the right answer to that).
5. **Path**: four weeks of pre-coding work (Section 5, items A1-A10: fix the rank sentence and the one-opening definition; run the within-cell tests that decide which race parameters the model gets; experience clock; data-set-specific reliability; NLSY79-CYA as a fourth public data set), then the model (3-4 months) specified as in Section 4, then REStud/JPE first with AEJ: Applied as the fall-back. Circulate the facts (Sections 1-6) as a working paper now; the fact is cheap to reproduce in the PSID and the clock is running.

## 3. Main remaining weaknesses a top-5 referee would raise

### 3.1 Identification and precision: which object is the fact?

The revision rescued the headline by moving it from family income to own earnings. The earnings opening is robust to everything: IPW 0.201\*\*\* (0.063), year FE 0.243\*\*\* (0.052), period-varying 0.235\*\*\* (0.087), 1990-2010 window 0.216\*\*\* (0.076), PPML 0.257\*\*\* (0.061), bottom tercile 0.232\* (0.127), re-weighted 0.188\* (0.100). The family-income opening, the object comparable with Chetty et al. (2020) and the one the title's "intergenerational persistence" evokes, is not: period-varying 0.168 (0.104; p=0.106), 1990-2010 window 0.084 (0.084; p=0.32), parental labor income 0.051 (0.045; p=0.26), below the median 0.023 (0.086). A referee will put these two lists side by side and ask: is this a paper about the transmission of men's wages (robust, new, and the paper's mechanism) or about intergenerational persistence of income (the title, the Chetty placement, Figure 1A, and fragile)? The paper should answer in the introduction: the fact is about sons' earnings and wages; family income shows the same profile and is reported because it is the literature's object, with its fragility stated in the same paragraph, not three sections later. Figure 1A should then plot earnings, not family income.

The pooled three-data-set earnings estimate with one year of parental income, 0.099\*\*\* (0.024), is the right headline precision. Two things weaken it. First, the three point estimates fall monotonically with the data set's attrition and cohort (PSID 0.151\*\*\*, NLSY79 0.088\*\*, NLSY97 0.058), and the data set with the lowest attrition (NLSY79, retention about 70-80 percent) has the smallest opening that is significant; the paper's explanation (parental-income measurement) is coherent but is not the only one, and the NLSY79 1978-80 mean is "close to one year" (log correlation 0.92, EVIDENCE 1.29), so the reliability story cannot be tested inside the NLSY79 on the paper's own account. Second, the reliability correction divides each data set by the PSID's ratios. The honest version computes each data set's own ratio (NLSY79 from 1978-80; NLSY97 from 1996-99 on the subsample that has it, with the selection stated) and reports the corrected opening with the ratio's sampling error propagated; a referee will otherwise say the correction is a rescaling by assumption.

A clean, cheap addition under the PI's constraints: the **NLSY79 Children and Young Adults** file is a fourth public data set with multi-year parental income (the mother's NLSY79 family income over many waves), the child's earnings to the early forties for cohorts born in the 1970s and 1980s, race, and the mother's AFQT. It answers the one-year objection directly, at the cost of a selected sample of mothers (young at the early births; say so and weight). The PI has the file. Two weeks.

### 3.2 Measurement

- **Age or potential experience?** White sons of richer parents are more often in graduate or professional school at 25-27; Figure 1A shows the White IGE at 0.16 at age 25 climbing to 0.40 by 31, which is the signature of late labor-market entry. The 25-29 band therefore compares Black sons with 3-7 years of experience to White sons from rich families with 0-3. The paper's own title says "the first decade of the career", so the clock should be experience. Two checks: (i) drop child-years in which the son is enrolled (PSID has enrollment for heads) or start the first band at 27; (ii) re-estimate the profile by potential experience (age - education - 6) in bands of five years, by race. If the White-Black gap is already open at 0-4 years of experience, "zero at 25-29" is partly a schooling-duration composition and the model's h_0 must carry it; if it is not, the fact is stronger. Two days. This is the measurement objection I would expect first from a Haider-Solon-literate referee, and it is not in the paper.
- **Attrition beyond observables.** IPW corrects selection on parental income and income at 25-29. Sixty percent of Black sons seen at 25-29 are gone by 45-49. If the Black sons who leave are those whose wages would have grown with parental income (movers, the upwardly mobile), IPW does nothing. The NLSY79, with far lower attrition, shows an opening of 0.088\*\* (0.045); the paper should say that this is the bound the low-attrition data set gives, and the NLSY79-CYA check above is the other half of the answer. One paragraph.
- **The Haider-Solon lambda.** Table 14 is a good addition: White lambda rises 0.335\*\*\* (0.046) from the bands below 35 to those above, Black 0.124\* (0.072), difference 0.211\*\* (0.086), kappa about zero. Two things to add: within parental terciles the White-Black difference in the rise is not significant (0.043 (0.173), 0.173 (0.134), 0.153 (0.144) for earnings, 41 C), which is consistent with low power and with the position reading; and the sample is 267 Black and 676 White sons with three valid years in each of three windows. The paper says this is "the definition of this object, not a test" (correct under the PI's rule 7); a referee will still want the within-tercile rows in the appendix table.
- **The return to schooling by age is confounded with the calendar.** The college premium rose from 1980 to 2000; a cohort observed at 25-29 in 1985 and at 40-44 in 2000 shows a rising "return to schooling with age" that is partly the period. Year fixed effects in Table 10 absorb levels, not the premium's trend. Add education x year (or education x a linear calendar trend) to columns 1-3, or estimate within a fixed cohort (born 1960-69). The racial difference in the rise is less exposed (both races are observed in the same years), but the Bhuller et al. framing of the level rise needs this. One day.
- **Table 3 pooled main-measure cell.** Reported with Q p=0.015; do not pool.

### 3.3 Interpretation

**(a) Race or position: the family-income dichotomy is over-read.** The paper's new sentence, "for sons' earnings the divergence is racial; for family income a large part of it is where in the parental distribution White families are", rests on: below-median opening 0.023 (0.086) for family income against 0.130 (0.087; p=0.136) for earnings; difference from the full sample -0.134\*\* (0.068; p=0.050) against -0.102 (0.070; p=0.145). The two below-median openings differ by 0.107 with a standard error near 0.12; the two differences-from-full-sample differ by 0.032. Nothing here separates earnings from family income; what the data say is that below the median, with 85 percent of Black and 46 percent of White sons, the power to estimate either opening is about 0.09 and both point estimates are smaller than the full-sample ones (and the re-weighted ones, 0.188\* (0.100) and 0.079 (0.087), likewise). The honest statement is: "re-weighting and the bottom half of the distribution leave the earnings opening at 0.13-0.23 and the family-income opening at 0.02-0.09, with standard errors of 0.09-0.13; the data do not establish whether the difference between the two outcomes is position or race". This matters for the model: it decides whether race enters a parameter or whether nonlinearity of access in x does the work (Section 4). Figure 2A supports the paper more than the regressions do (the White 40-49 curve is steeper than the 25-29 curve across the whole common range, including vigintiles 1-6; the Black 40-49 curve above vigintile 6 is drawn on 15-33 sons per point), and the text should lean on the figure, with the cell sizes.

**(b) Schooling: the sign of the employer-learning story.** Sec. 5.4 offers employer learning (Altonji-Pierret; Lange; Arcidiacono-Bayer-Hizmo) as a reading of "Black sons' higher return to schooling at 25-29 and its flat profile". Under employer learning the coefficient on an easily observed signal (schooling) falls with experience as productivity is revealed and the coefficient on hard-to-observe ability (AFQT) rises. The paper's White profile is a schooling return that rises from 0.049\*\*\* (0.005) to 0.128\*\*\* (0.008) in wages; that is the opposite of learning about schooling and is the Mincer/Ben-Porath complementarity of schooling with on-the-job accumulation, or sorting of the educated into jobs with steep profiles (which Table 9 says is half of it). The Black profile (higher at entry, 0.076\*\*\* (0.007), flatter) is closer to what learning predicts. The test that separates them is in the NLSY79 and is one regression: the return to AFQT and to schooling by age band and race (the Arcidiacono-Bayer-Hizmo design run by race, with Pinkston 2006 and Neal-Johnson 1996 as the reference points). Table 9 already shows that AFQT carries growth for White men (0.009\*\*\* (0.003)) and not for Black men (0.003 (0.005)) before education is controlled; the age profile of the AFQT return by race is the missing piece. One day; it will also tell the model whether gamma should load on e or on an ability index.

Note also that the Black return to schooling at 25-29 is above the White one (earnings 0.124\*\*\* (0.010) vs 0.045\*\*\* (0.007); difference 0.079\*\*\* (0.012)). The model of Section 7 has no parameter that produces a race difference in the entry return to schooling; it has one that produces a race difference in its growth. Either h_0 depends on e by race, or the model will miss the 25-29 moment and push the misfit into gamma_1(r). Section 4.

**(c) "Built in wage growth with that employer".** The abstract attributes the divergence to the starting job and to "wage growth with that employer". For the pooled and White gradients that is right; for the racial difference, the within-job part is -0.012\* (0.007; p=0.084) of the -0.021\*\*\* (0.008) growth-gradient gap at 25-34 (Table 11), and within cells the racial difference is -0.006 (0.012) / -0.007 (0.011) (Table 9). The abstract should say "in the job a son holds then and, for White sons, in wage growth within it".

**(d) Fathers' occupations.** Beyond the "White phenomenon" sentence (Section 2 table), two points. The rich-White-minus-rich-Black contrast (0.117\*\*\* (0.038)) comes from a regression with race x tercile cells and a linear parental-income term; in that same regression rich White minus poor White is -0.086 (0.061) (43, Section 2), so the contrast is the racial gap in following the father's occupation at equal parental income within the top tercile, not evidence that class amplifies it. Say that. And the mean level of following differs by race at every age (22.4 vs 15.3 percent at 35-55), which is itself a result worth one sentence (rule 4: do not fold it away because it is a level).

**(e) The displacement result is equal by race and belongs in the text, not only in Table 11.** P(displaced) falls with parental income by -0.037\*\*\* (0.009) for White and -0.038\*\*\* (0.014) for Black sons at 25-34. That is a precise statement that family background buys the same protection from job loss in both races; it is a finding about what parental income does, and it pins delta's dependence on x in the model. One sentence in Sec. 5.5 already notes it; promote it to the summary paragraph.

### 3.4 Writing and length

- Abstract: 210 words; cut to 150 (QJE) or 100 (AER). The cut is the robustness sentence (move to the intro) and the fathers' sentence.

## 4. The model (Section 7)

### 4.1 Is the specification right given the evidence?

The spine is right and parsimonious: wages, not hours; background through schooling and access; accumulation that depends on schooling and on the job type; a match component lost at displacement; depreciation out of work; race in the level, in displacement risk, in access, and in the pay-off of schooling in accumulation. Three things are wrong or missing.

**(i) The competing model is dismissed in one sentence, and the sentence is wrong.** Sec. 7 says models in which background acts only at entry "imply, when wage profiles after entry are common to all backgrounds, an IGE that changes little with age". The qualifier does the work: in Huggett-Ventura-Yaron (2011) and in any Ben-Porath model with heterogeneous learning ability, profiles after entry are not common, dispersion of earnings rises with age, and if learning ability is correlated with parental income the log IGE rises with age with no jobs, no access and no race in the technology. The paper's model must say why a job-side block (access, job types) is needed in addition to a worker-side block (gamma depends on e). The evidence that separates them is exactly the within-cell evidence: half of the White same-employer gradient is sorting into steep cells (Table 9), the early cell accounts for 0.49\*\*\* of the divergence (Table 7), access differs by race at equal parental income and education (0.035\*, Table 8), and Black sons of richer parents gain less at job moves (-0.069\*\* (0.028), Table 11). A pure learning-ability model produces none of the last three. Write this paragraph; it is the model's reason to exist.

**(ii) The two race parameters are not yet shown to be separately identified from access.** Suppose only access differs by race (p_0 lower for Black sons at given x, e) and gamma_G > gamma_O with gamma_1 common. Then the return to schooling rises faster with age for White sons (more of the educated are in G jobs), the education gradient of same-employer growth is larger for White sons (same reason), and education "accounts for" a share of the divergence, all without gamma_1(r). The moments the paper lists for gamma_1(r) (Tables 9 and 10) are therefore not sufficient; the moments that separate gamma_1(r) from access are the same regressions **within the early cell**: the return to schooling by age band and race with fixed effects for the son's cell at 25-34, and the education gradient of same-employer growth by race within the cell of the first interview. If the racial differences survive within cells (-0.007\*\* and -0.055\*\*\* or their within-cell analogues), gamma_1(r) is a parameter; if they vanish, race enters access only and gamma_1 is common. These are two regressions on existing scripts (35, 36, 42), three days, and they decide the model's structure before a line of SMM code is written. The parsimony rule says: run them, then give the model only the race parameters that survive.

**(iii) Three pieces of reduced-form flexibility and one missing parameter.**
- p_0(x, e, r) is left as a function. Give it a form: logit with a linear index a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 e + a_3 1[Black]. No race x x interaction: the racial difference in the parental-income gradient of access, -0.055\*\*\* (0.015) in Table 8 column (1), is 0.022 (0.024) once education and the father's job are in (column 5). Nonlinearity in x comes from the logit and absorbs the position reading of 3.3(a).
- Use the same access function for offers on the job: an outside offer is of type G with the same probability p(x, e, r) as at entry. One race parameter then governs access throughout the career and generates lower gains at moves for Black sons of richer parents (Table 11) without a race-specific search friction. Add a race-specific search friction only if the move rates themselves (P(moved) is 0.125 Black vs 0.130 White at 25-34, 42 C) cannot be matched; on these numbers they can. If it is added, cite Bowlus-Eckstein (2002), which is the model it becomes.
- The early-career transfer has no moment anywhere in the paper. Under the PI's rule it is not estimated: drop it, or add one moment (the PSID wealth supplements 1984-2021 report inheritances and large gifts received; the 2013 Rosters and Transfers module reports parental help) and keep it. Dropping is the parsimonious choice; the paper's own evidence (parental labor income reproduces the earnings opening, 0.134\*\*\*) says transfers are not where the earnings divergence comes from.
- The entry return to schooling differs by race (3.3(b)). Allow h_0 = b_0 + b_1(r) e + nu, with b_1(r) identified by the 25-29 return to schooling by race (0.076\*\*\* vs 0.049\*\*\* in wages), and let the sensitivity matrix say whether it is needed. Without it, the 25-29 moment misfits and gamma_1(r) absorbs the misfit, which would make the subsidy counterfactual wrong in a known direction.
- Education is exogenous (e = e(x, r) + nu). Then "an education subsidy that equalises schooling" is a shift of e, not a policy. Either call it what it is ("equalising schooling") or add a schooling choice (a cost of schooling in x and r, as in Abbott et al. 2019), which adds two or three parameters and a forward-looking valuation. Given four months, keep e exogenous and rename the experiment; say in one sentence why.
- mu_r is held fixed in every counterfactual; say so, and say what it is (the racial wage-level gap at equal h, the discrimination/pre-market residual of Neal-Johnson). The rank intercept gap of 11-15 percentiles is mostly mu_r, and no experiment in the list touches it; a reader will want to know that the paper's experiments are about the slope by construction.

## 6. Risks, fallbacks and journal targets

1. **A3 shows the racial difference in the schooling pay-off vanishes within cells.** Likelihood moderate (Table 9's within-cell racial differences are already -0.006 / -0.007, n.s.). Then the model has race in mu, delta, e and access only; the schooling finding is re-read as "schooling buys access to the jobs in which human capital accumulates, and that access differs by race"; the subsidy counterfactual becomes more, not less, interesting (equalising schooling without equalising access leaves the slope gap). The paper is better specified, not weaker.
2. **A5 shows the gap is open at 0-4 years of potential experience.** Then "zero at 25-29" is partly schooling duration; the headline becomes "the gap in persistence is built over the first decade of experience" and h_0 carries a race x e term. The policy message (scored too early) survives; the Chetty-window sentence weakens.
3. **The NLSY79-CYA shows a smaller opening with multi-year parental income.** Then the reliability explanation of the PSID-NLSY difference loses its main support, and the paper must state the opening as 0.06-0.10 in the NLSYs and 0.15-0.23 in the PSID and let the reader weigh them. Still publishable at AEJ: Applied on the pooled fact plus the model; the race-specific statements become "concentrated among White sons in the PSID".
4. **The family-income fact is read as position.** Already half-conceded. Fallback: the paper is about men's earnings and wages (robust), with family income as the comparison object; the title keeps "persistence" only if Figure 1A shows earnings.
5. **The model cannot separate access from gamma_1(r) (collinear Jacobian columns).** Fallback is the nested model B with access only, and counterfactuals reported as bounds between B and C.
6. **The model takes longer than four months.** Submit the facts paper (Sections 1-6 after A1-A10) to AEJ: Applied with Section 7 as a specified plan and the within-cell identification evidence included; keep the estimated model for the companion structural paper. The fact must not be lost to a scoop while the model is debugged.
7. **A referee demands administrative confirmation.** Answer: the three public data sets plus the CYA, the prediction for the IRS cohorts, and the statement that the paper's object (the log IGE by race and age) is not published anywhere from administrative data.

**Journal targets.** As the paper stands after A1-A10 and without the model: JOLE / JHR / EJ with high probability; AEJ: Applied about one in three. With the model estimated as in Section 4 and the race parameters identified within cells: AEJ: Applied or JEEA as the modal outcome (about one in two), REStud or JPE about one in four, AER/QJE about one in ten. Submit to REStud or JPE first; the age-35 policy metric and the nested-model identification table are what those editors buy, and the fall to AEJ: Applied costs four months, not the paper.
