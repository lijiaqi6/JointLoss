A father's childhood layoff moves his sons' and his daughters' pay in opposite directions. In
one reduced specification for both sexes, a father's teen layoff changes his son's hourly wage between 25--29 and 45--49
by $-0.538^{**}$ and his daughter's by $+0.387^{*}$; within the same person the slopes are $-0.086$ (s.e.\ 0.096) and
$+0.168^{**}$ ($p = 0.025$) (panel B of Table~\ref{tab:layoff_exog}). Stacking sons and daughters and testing the
difference directly, it is 0.925$^{***}$ ($p = 0.001$) in the wage change and 1.294$^{***}$ in the earnings change, and
0.254$^{**}$ ($p = 0.049$) and 0.380$^{**}$ ($p = 0.030$) a decade within person. With one job-end net in every wave the
change differences are 1.295$^{**}$ and 1.753$^{**}$. The difference is positive in all three birth cohorts; the
daughters' own gain is clear in those born before 1963 (0.221$^{*}$) and from 1976 (0.644$^{***}$) and nil in those born
1963--75.\footnote{Sons and daughters of the same family share the cluster. Code: \texttt{107.LayoffSexDifference.py}.}
A trait of the father passed on to all his children would lower both profiles. The two change differences survive a
family-wise correction (adjusted $p$ 0.004 and 0.003); the within-person ones are borderline (0.061). The daughters' gain
is not selection into who is observed: the father's layoff does not predict whether a daughter is observed with a wage at
25--29 or 45--49, and weighting by the inverse of that probability leaves her slope at 0.176$^{**}$. It does not run
through the mother: she does not work more in the father's layoff waves (0.011, s.e.\ 0.010), and the daughters' gain is
the same whether she did or not. Schooling moves apart: a teen layoff goes with 0.89 more years of schooling for
daughters than for sons ($p = 0.069$) (panel C of Table~\ref{tab:layoff_referee}). Nor is it selection into
work or marriage: their employment does not change, and the within-person wage slope is $0.169^{**}$ with partnership
and full-year work held fixed. It does not reach their earnings with zero years kept (a change of 0.188, s.e.\ 0.418,
in a Poisson model, against $-0.523^{**}$ for sons), and their family income rises at 45--49 because their partners earn
more ($0.677^{***}$) (panel D of Table~\ref{tab:layoff_mechanism}). Why daughters of laid-off fathers are paid more when
they work, and partner with higher earners, the data here do not explain.\footnote{145 daughters with a teen layoff year
are observed at 45--49. Code: \texttt{92.LayoffDaughters.py}, \texttt{105.DaughtersMechanism.py}.}
