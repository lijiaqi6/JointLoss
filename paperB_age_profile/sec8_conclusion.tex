\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

A father's layoff during his son's childhood goes with no cost to the son in his late twenties and with earnings lower
by about 11 log points per layoff year in his late forties, in his wage and within his occupation. The cost is
negative in every birth cohort and under one rule for recording job ends, follows layoffs in childhood rather than after the son is 20, and is
absent after plant closures and national declines of the father's industry. Its point estimates are largest after the layoffs that mark
unstable employment---repeated, with small income losses, in fathers who keep losing jobs---rather than the large
income shocks,
and layoffs in bad times carry about half of it, although they cost the father at least as much earnings: what they do not cost him is work, so the son's cost follows the father's employment, not his income loss, as far as differences that are not
significant can say. It does not come with the father's job: it holds at equal sector,
occupation and union membership, in every sector, and after recalls as after moves to a new employer; nor does it come
from self-employed fathers in the comparison group. Sons of laid-off fathers are more often self-employed, more so at 45--49
than at 25--29, and self-employment pays them less with age: the cost sits in their self-employed years, in earnings
and in the hourly wage, and not in their years as employees. The same layoffs raise daughters' wages, and that
difference is not selection into who is observed, into work or into marriage.

The result is one of the components of the IGE whose weight grows with the child's age. The parents'
wage and, where it is measured, their wealth carry between two fifths and a half of the IGE, which rises with age in
the son's own earnings and not in his spouse's or in asset income; the son's measured skill carries three fifths of its rise
with age in the NLSY79 and a quarter in the NLSY97, twenty years later; and the son's own spells carry much of the IGE in his twenties and little
after. The IGE rises with age because the components that grow, among them the father's layoffs, outweigh the one that
fades.

Wealth adds two results. What the parents' wealth carries is housing---the home and other real estate---not a family business or
financial assets. And wealth has its own age profile: the son's net worth follows his parents' more closely until his
late thirties, after his earnings ranks have stopped rising, and not through gifts or inheritances.

Two implications follow for policy. The cost of a father's layoffs does not follow the income they take, and insurance that
replaces income, as legislated and as simulated at the father's wage, is not shown to weaken it; if it is what
instability does to the son, the margin is the stability of the father's employment and support for the son as he
finishes school and starts work, and if it marks the father, it tells policy which children to reach. And the age at
which children's earnings are observed changes what a childhood shock or policy appears to do: scored at 25--29, a
father's layoff looks costless, and exposure to the EITC shows a similar age profile even in families the credit barely reaches, a warning
about schedule-based designs scored at one age.

The rise of the IGE with age is White, and the components locate the racial difference in the father's schooling, which
pays off for White sons, and in who is laid off, not in what a layoff does. Three questions remain open: whether the cost
of a father's unstable employment comes from what it does to his son or from what it reveals about the father, which no
design with public US data can settle without a layoff the father did not choose and enough power; what lowers these
sons' pay within their occupation in their forties; and why the daughters of the same fathers are paid more when they
work.
