\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

What parents pass on through the IGE of earnings is first their wage, their schooling and, where it is measured, their
wealth, then their spells out of work, and least their hours and their place. The components differ in when they show.
The parents' wage and the father's schooling show from the son's mid-thirties, through skill and schooling that pay more
as human capital accumulates on the job, in two cohorts twenty years apart. A father's layoff during the son's
childhood shows only after 40, in the son's wage, while a plant closure or a decline of the father's industry shows at
no age; the same layoff raises his daughter's wage with age. The son's own spells carry much of the IGE in his twenties and little after. The IGE rises with age because the
components that grow outweigh the one that fades.

Each component implies a different policy and a different age at which to judge it. If the wage and schooling are
what is passed on, schooling and training act on it, and their effect on persistence appears after 35. The spells that
are passed on are the father's repeated layoffs with small income losses, not the large income shocks, and insurance
that replaces income, as legislated and simulated at the father's wage, does not weaken them: what they call for is
stability of the father's employment, not income replacement alone. If the group's labor market is passed on, policy
aimed at groups acts on it, but in these data it works only between races and cannot be told apart from anything else
that differs by race. Hours carry little, so incentives to work more are not where the IGE is. In each case an
evaluation that scores the son's earnings around 30 sees the early components and misses the late ones.

The rise of the IGE with age is White, and the components locate the racial difference: in the father's schooling,
which pays off for White sons, and in who is laid off, not in what a layoff does. The Black IGE is carried by family
structure. Three questions remain open. Whether the cost of a father's unstable employment comes from what it does to
his son or from a trait of fathers laid off early in their sons' lives: layoffs in bad times carry half of it, and no
design with public US data assigns a layoff the father did not choose with enough power to say more. What lowers these
sons' pay within their occupation in their forties. And why the daughters of the same fathers are paid more when they
work.
