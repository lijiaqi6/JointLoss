\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

A father's layoff during his son's childhood costs the son nothing in his late twenties and about 11 log points of
earnings per layoff year in his late forties, in his wage and within his occupation. The cost holds in every birth
cohort and under one rule for recording job ends, follows layoffs in childhood rather than after the son is 20, and is
absent after plant closures and national declines of the father's industry. It follows the layoffs that mark unstable
employment---repeated, with small income losses, in fathers who keep losing jobs---rather than the large income shocks,
and layoffs in bad times carry about half of it. Sons of laid-off fathers are more often self-employed, more so at 45--49
than at 25--29, and self-employment pays them less with age: the cost sits in their self-employed years, in earnings
and in the hourly wage, and not in their years as employees. The same layoffs raise daughters' wages, and that
difference is not selection into who is observed, into work or into marriage.

The result is the one part of what parents pass on whose weight grows with the child's age. The parents' wage and,
where it is measured, their wealth carry about half of the IGE; the son's skill at entry carries most of its rise with age, in
two cohorts twenty years apart; and the son's own spells carry much of the IGE in his twenties and little after. The IGE
rises with age because the components that grow, among them the father's layoffs, outweigh the one that fades.

Two implications follow for policy. The cost of a father's layoffs is not the income they take, and insurance that
replaces income, as legislated and as simulated at the father's wage, is not shown to weaken it; if it is what
instability does to the son, the margin is the stability of the father's employment and support for the son as he
finishes school and starts work, and if it marks the father, it tells policy which children to reach. And the age at
which children's earnings are observed changes what a childhood shock or policy appears to do: scored at 25--29, a
father's layoff looks costless, and exposure to the EITC goes with somewhat lower earnings that disappear by 40--44.

The rise of the IGE with age is White, and the components locate the racial difference in the father's schooling, which
pays off for White sons, and in who is laid off, not in what a layoff does. Three questions remain open: whether the cost
of a father's unstable employment comes from what it does to his son or from what it reveals about the father, which no
design with public US data can settle without a layoff the father did not choose and enough power; what lowers these
sons' pay within their occupation in their forties; and why the daughters of the same fathers are paid more when they
work.
