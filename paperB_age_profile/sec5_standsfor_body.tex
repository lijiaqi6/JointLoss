
A layoff selects fathers, and a trait that the father passes on and that pays off more with the son's age---Section~\ref{sec:ageprofile}
shows that skill does---would produce the same profile; son fixed effects do not remove a trait whose return grows.
The designs below narrow the reading without assigning the layoff. Together they say that the cost follows a father's
layoffs during his son's childhood when they come from his own unstable employment, not from shocks to his plant or
industry, and that it is not the income the layoff costs. Whether the instability does the damage or marks a father
whose sons fare worse later, no design with public US data can say.

\paragraph{Shocks the father did not choose.} Plant closures carry no cost (Section~\ref{sec:stability}), and neither
does a national decline of the father's industry during the son's teens, which raises the father's unemployment. With
sector and birth-year fixed effects, the share of the son's 12--18 in which national employment of the father's sector
fell raises the father's unemployment share by 0.080$^{**}$ ($p = 0.014$) and his layoff share by 0.030$^{*}$ ($p =
0.086$; $F = 2.9$), and the son's earnings profile does not bend: the within-son reduced form is 0.143$^{**}$, of the
wrong sign for a cost, and the placebo windows at 5--11 and 19--25 are zero (panel A of Table~\ref{tab:layoff_exog}).
The positive sign is the coarseness of fifteen sectors: with the father's detailed industry interacted with age it falls
to 0.067 (s.e.\ 0.076), while sons do not avoid their fathers' declining sectors ($-0.054$, s.e.\ 0.052) and the national
labor market at their entry does not carry it.\footnote{Code: \texttt{112.IndustryShockGain.py}.} The
first stage on layoffs is too weak to test the layoff cost itself: the cost the layoff path would imply,
$0.030 \times -0.24$, is a tenth of the reduced form's standard error. What the design shows is that demand-driven
unemployment of the father, like a plant closure, is not passed on.\footnote{Father's sector: his 3-digit industry in the
earliest wave at the son's 10--13 (85 percent) or his first teen wave, in 15 sectors; national employment from the BLS
Current Employment Statistics, 1939--2025; 3,145 sons, 632 sector $\times$ birth-year clusters. Code:
\texttt{93.LayoffIndustryShocks.py}.}

\paragraph{What kind of layoff.} The point estimates put the cost in the layoffs that look like unstable employment,
not in the costly income shocks, though no split is significant at 5 percent. Splitting the teen layoff share into parts that add up to it, the within-son cost is $-0.473^{***}$ for
layoffs with a small fall in family income and $-0.096$ for those with a large fall ($p = 0.079$ for equality);
$-0.338^{***}$ for layoffs followed by fewer than 500 hours of unemployment and 0.225 for longer spells ($p = 0.064$);
and $-0.268^{**}$ for sons whose father was laid off in two or more teen years against 0.295 for one ($p = 0.145$)
(panel D of Table~\ref{tab:layoff_selection}). Parental income at the son's 20--26, the money after the teens that the
13--19 measure leaves out, does not move it ($-0.232^{**}$ against $-0.236^{**}$). Nor does the family's wealth: teen
layoffs leave the parents somewhat less wealthy when the son is 20--30 (by about 14 percent per layoff year, $p = 0.060$),
but holding their later net worth fixed leaves the son's within-son cost where it is on that sample ($-0.175$, s.e.\ 0.123,
before and after), where the cost is itself imprecise.\footnote{PSID net worth with home
equity, 1984 on; 2,795 sons with the parents' wealth at 20--30. Code: \texttt{117.LayoffWealth.py}.} If the cost were
the income or the wealth a layoff takes, the large losses and the long spells would carry it.

\paragraph{What kind of father.} The costly layoff marks a father whose employment stays unstable. When his son is
20--24, a unit of teen layoff share goes with more years unemployed (0.289$^{***}$) and more new layoffs (0.132$^{***}$),
at equal parental income and the father's own teen-window wage and hours, and with somewhat lower earnings
($-0.193^{*}$); his employment does not change. The small-loss layoffs, which carry the son's cost, are the ones
followed by new layoffs (0.220$^{***}$, against 0.057 after a large loss) (panel A of Table~\ref{tab:layoff_mechanism}).

\paragraph{The father or his job.} The cost is not a property of the father's job. Repeated layoffs with small income
losses and short unemployment are also the signature of temporary layoffs with recall in cyclical industries, and if
the cost came with such a job it would sit in recalls and in some sectors. It does neither. Holding the father's sector
(16 groups), occupation (nine groups) and union membership fixed, each interacted with the son's age, leaves the
within-son cost at $-0.226^{**}$ ($p = 0.023$) and the change between 25--29 and 45--49 at $-0.874^{***}$ ($p<0.001$),
against $-0.236^{**}$ and $-0.914^{***}$. The within-son cost is the same in every sector ($p = 0.98$ for equality),
from $-0.143$ in trade and services to $-0.282^{*}$ in construction and mining. And it follows layoffs after which the
father went back to an employer he had before ($-0.251^{*}$, $p = 0.052$) as much as layoffs after which he worked for a
new one ($-0.153$; $p = 0.67$ for the difference).\footnote{A layoff is a recall when the father's tenure with his
employer at a later interview, within three years, goes back past the year the job ended, and a new-employer layoff
when it starts after it or he is self-employed; tenure is in months from 1976 and in categories before (none in 1974
and 1978). Of 797 teen layoff years, 194 are recalls, 345 new-employer layoffs and 258 unknown. The classification is
noisy: for the spells of 1980--82, for which the PSID asked whether the man returned to the same employer, it calls 41
of 76 returns recalls and 48 of 402 non-returns recalls, so the recall--new difference is attenuated. Union membership
1969--72 and 1975 (member) and 1976 on (member or covered). Code: \texttt{120.LayoffRecall.py}
(\texttt{output/layoff\_recall.md}).}

\paragraph{Before and after the layoff.} The fathers were not already on a falling path. In an event study around a
father's first layoff in his son's teens, with father and calendar-year fixed effects, his earnings in the six years
before are not significantly different from the year before (jointly $p = 0.20$), though they are about 0.1 higher three
to six years before; they then fall by 0.198$^{***}$ in the layoff year and remain 0.138$^{**}$ lower six years later, the
persistent loss of the displacement literature \citep{stevens1997persistent}. His employment shows no pre-trend ($p =
0.36$) (panel A of Table~\ref{tab:layoff_referee}). The same fathers' layoffs after the son is 26 carry nothing for the
son (0.063, s.e.\ 0.128); the fathers still observed then are a subsample on which the teen layoff's cost is itself
imprecise ($-0.124$, s.e.\ 0.122), so this placebo is weak.\footnote{2,238 sons whose father is a head in at least three
waves at their 27--35. Code: \texttt{108.LayoffRefereeChecks.py}.}

\paragraph{Layoffs in bad times.} Layoffs that fall when the father's sector or his state is in decline, and so say
less about him \citep{gibbons1991layoffs}, still cost the son, about half as much. Classing each teen layoff year as bad
times when national employment of the father's sector fell or his state's unemployment rate was at least a point above
its mean (53 percent of layoff years, against 44 percent of all window years), the change between 25--29 and 45--49 is
$-0.675^{**}$ ($p = 0.035$) for bad-times layoffs and $-1.400^{***}$ for the others; within the same son $-0.166$ ($p =
0.27$) and $-0.408^{***}$; the two do not differ significantly ($p = 0.26$; panel B of
Table~\ref{tab:layoff_mechanism}).\footnote{With the sector's decline alone: $-0.664^{*}$ against $-1.143^{***}$, $p =
0.66$ for equality within son. Code: \texttt{104.FatherPathRecession.py}.} The cost is therefore not selection alone, but
the layoffs that are the father's own carry more of it.

The point estimates say that the son's cost follows the father's employment, not his earnings loss, though none of
the differences below is significant. Displaced workers lose more when
displaced in a recession \citep{davis2011recessions}, and so do these fathers: over the six years after his first
teen-window layoff the father's earnings are lower by 0.230$^{***}$ after a bad-times layoff and by 0.130$^{**}$ after
another one (difference $-0.099$, $p = 0.25$). His employment falls only after the other layoffs, by 0.079$^{**}$
($p = 0.027$), against 0.016 ($p = 0.30$) in bad times (difference $p = 0.11$). The layoffs that cost the son less cost the father at least as
much income, and less work. No single recession carries the son's cost: most of it is in layoffs outside recessions
($-0.383^{***}$ within son; 305 treated sons, 92 of them at 45--49), and the six recessions from 1970 to 2008--09 do not
differ from one another ($p = 0.21$).\footnote{Event study with father fixed effects, calendar-year and age dummies,
event time $-6$ to $+6$ split by the times of the father's first teen-window layoff (163 bad, 155 other); no pre-trend in
either (mean of $-6$ to $-2$: 0.083 and 0.053). Recession parts: each teen layoff year assigned to the NBER recession of
its layoff record (1970; 1974--75; 1980--82; 1990--91; 2001; 2008--09); sons whose teens fell in 2001 or 2008--09 are
not yet observed at 45--49. Code: \texttt{124.RecessionLayoffs.py} (\texttt{output/recession\_layoffs.md}).}

\paragraph{Which parental income passes on.} Income tied to layoffs passes on more strongly than other parental
income, and income lost to plant closures does not; this restates the layoff result in the terms of
\citet{shea2000does}. Splitting log parental income into the part predicted by the father's layoffs and the rest, the
predicted part passes on with an elasticity of 0.742$^{***}$ against 0.381$^{***}$ (difference 0.361$^{**}$, $p =
0.016$); the part predicted by plant closures, 0.074 (s.e.\ 0.561). With one predictor the first coefficient is the
layoff's association with the son's earnings divided by its gradient in parental income, so it is not a separate
finding; what it adds is the comparison with Shea, who instruments parental income with sources beyond the parents'
control and finds that money as such matters little. The closure row agrees with him; the layoff row says that what
passes on strongly is what comes with a layoff, not the income it costs.\footnote{Appendix Table~\ref{tab:shea_spell}
has the split for the father's and mother's unemployment and for the son's wage and family income. Code:
\texttt{74.FrictionIGEPooled.py}, \texttt{89.SheaContrastBySpell.py}.}
