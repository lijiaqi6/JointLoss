\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A father's layoff during his son's childhood costs the son nothing in his late twenties and a great deal in his
forties. In the Panel Study of Income Dynamics (PSID), at equal parental income and equal values of the parents' wages,
hours and other spells, a father's layoff in his son's teens is unrelated to the son's earnings at 25--29 and lowers
them by 0.110$^{***}$ log points per layoff year at 45--49, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:layoffbands}. The cost is in the
son's hourly wage, it grows within the same son ($-0.236^{**}$ per ten years of age per unit share), it holds in every
birth cohort and is larger when the PSID's job-end question is applied the same way in every wave, and it follows
layoffs during the son's childhood rather than after he is 20. A plant closure, which displaces workers whatever their
quality \citep{gibbons1991layoffs}, carries no detectable cost at any age, and the layoff's late cost exceeds the
closure's by $-0.914^{***}$. The same layoffs pull daughters the other way: their
wages rise with age, a difference from sons of 0.925$^{***}$ per unit share between 25--29 and 45--49 ($p = 0.001$).

The result explains part of a known fact and changes how it should be read. The intergenerational elasticity of
earnings (IGE) rises with the age at which children are observed, from 0.28 at 25--29 to 0.47 at 40--44
\citep{haider2006life, nybom2016heterogeneous}. Splitting it into what parents do shows that the parents' wage and, where
it is measured, their wealth carry between two fifths and a half of it, and that the son's skill at entry carries most
of its rise: in the NLSY79 his AFQT carries three fifths of the rise, and its contribution rises by the same factor
twenty years later in the NLSY97, so the rise is age and not the rising skill premium. One parental component's share
rises with age, the parents' spells out of work, and it is the father's layoffs.

It is housing. The parents' net worth carries between a seventh and three tenths of the IGE, depending on how it
enters (14.5\%$^{***}$ as the inverse hyperbolic sine, 29.1\%$^{***}$ as a rank), and split into its parts it is the
family home: housing equity carries 20.9\%$^{***}$, business and farm equity 2.0 percent and financial and other assets
5.6 percent.

Wealth has its own age profile. The rank--rank slope of the son's net worth on his parents' keeps rising into his late
thirties, from 0.228$^{***}$ at 25--29 to 0.408$^{***}$ at 35--39 (trend 0.089$^{***}$ a decade), after his earnings
ranks have stopped rising (trend 0.016, $p = 0.50$, on the same son-years); and it is not gifts or inheritances
arriving later, which follow the parents' wealth at every age without rising.

What the layoff stands for is the paper's central question, and the answer is unstable employment rather than lost
income. Shocks the father did not choose carry no cost: neither plant closures nor national declines of his industry,
which raise his unemployment. The costly layoffs are repeated ones with small income losses and short unemployment, in
fathers who keep losing jobs after the son has left home; before the first layoff the father's earnings and employment
show no significant pre-trend, and six years after it his earnings are still 0.14 log points lower. The instability is
the father's and not his job's: the cost holds at equal sector, occupation and union membership of the father, is the
same in every sector, and follows layoffs after which he returned to a former employer as much as those after which he
moved to a new one. Parental income after the son's teens does not carry the cost,
and the income that layoffs predict passes on twice as strongly as other parental income while the income closures
predict does not, the contrast \citet{shea2000does} draws between parents' money and what comes with it. No design with
public US data assigns a layoff the father did not choose with enough power to say whether the cost is what the
instability does to the son or what it reveals about the father; the paper says which reading each test supports.

What bad-times layoffs don't cost the father is work, so the son's cost follows the father's employment, not his
income loss. Layoffs when the father's sector or state is in decline say less about him, and they cost the son about
half as much as other layoffs. They cost the father at least as much earnings---over the six years after the layoff,
$-0.230^{***}$ against $-0.130^{**}$---but no employment: $-0.016$ against $-0.079^{**}$ after the other layoffs.

In the son's career the cost is a lower wage within his occupation in his forties. Two thirds of it is within the
occupation and a quarter a lower-paid occupation. It is not where he starts---a teen layoff barely changes his chance of
a good first job, and holding his early job fixed leaves the cost intact---and it is not his own instability: his
displacements, unemployment and job changes do not move with his father's layoffs.

Self-employment rises: sons of laid-off fathers are more often self-employed, by 0.097$^{**}$ at 25--29 and 0.271$^{**}$
at 45--49 per unit share of teen layoff years.

And self-employment pays them less with age. When self-employed, their earnings fall behind steeply: within the same
son the earnings profile bends by $-0.885^{***}$ a decade in his self-employed years and by $-0.084$ in his years as an
employee, and the hourly wage shows the same contrast ($-0.609^{**}$ against $-0.004$). It is not business income
moved out of earnings: with the asset part of business income added, the cost in self-employed years is
$-0.812^{***}$. Neither his health, nor where he
lives, nor his family's wealth carries the cost, and schooling carries a sixth of it.

The daughters' gain is not selection. The father's layoff does not predict whether a daughter is observed with a wage,
and weighting for it leaves her gain unchanged; it survives holding her employment and partnership fixed; it does not
run through the mother, who does not work more after the father's layoff. Schooling moves apart---daughters of laid-off
fathers get 0.89 more years than sons ($p = 0.069$)---and they partner with men who earn more. Whatever the layoff marks
or does, it is not a trait passed on to all of a father's children alike.

The results bear on policy in two ways. Insurance is not shown to weaken the cost: neither the generosity of the state's
unemployment insurance, nor the benefit the father's own wage would have drawn, nor the weeks of benefit available
change it, though these tests can exclude only effects larger than the cost itself; this fits a cost that comes from
unstable employment rather than from the income lost. And the age at which children's outcomes are observed matters:
evaluations that score them early \citep{bastian2018long, chetty2016effects}, or pool them across ages
\citep{hoynes2016long, jackson2016effects}, weight lightly the components that appear after 40. The EITC is a caution, not an example: a family's maximum credit during the son's teens goes with
earnings lower by 0.016 ($p = 0.052$) per thousand dollars at 25--29 and with faster growth after, but the same profile
appears in families the credit barely reaches, and the credit, a few hundred dollars a year, is too small to move the
IGE by more than about 0.01; the data find no change in it.

The rise of the IGE with age is White. The father's schooling carries 0.065$^{***}$ of the White IGE and 0.002 of the
Black IGE; the cost of a layoff to Black sons cannot be distinguished from the cost to White sons, but Black fathers'
layoffs are not concentrated in low-income families, so the rising cost raises the White IGE and not the Black one.

\paragraph{Contributions and literature.} The paper contributes to three literatures. First, it adds the age of the
child to the evidence on parental job loss, which estimates the effect of one shock at one age
\citep{oreopoulos2008intergenerational, hilger2016parental, kalil2011parental}: the cost of a father's layoff appears
after 40, beyond the ages those studies observe, plant closures carry none at any age, and sons and daughters move in
opposite directions. Second, it splits the IGE on the parents' side, by the child's age. Earlier accounts split it on the
child's side, into the child's schooling, skills and attachment to work \citep{blanden2007accounting,
gregg2019intergenerational, rothstein2019inequality}, model the joint transmission of wages and hours
\citep{altonji2000intergenerational}, or ask whether parents' money matters \citep{shea2000does,
cardak2013intergenerational, black2005why}; here the parents' wage, hours, spells, place and wealth are components of one
exact decomposition \citep{gelbach2016covariates}, replicated in four data sets. Third, it connects the life-cycle bias
of the IGE \citep{haider2006life, grawe2006lifecycle, nybom2016heterogeneous, nybom2017biases, chetty2014where} to what
is passed on: the bias is the rising pay-off of skill, the delayed cost of a father's layoff and the fading weight of the
son's own spells.
