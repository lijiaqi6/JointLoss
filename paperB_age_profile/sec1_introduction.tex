\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The intergenerational elasticity of earnings (IGE) is one number for many things a parent passes on, and it changes
with the age at which children are observed. In the Panel Study of Income Dynamics (PSID) the elasticity of sons'
earnings with respect to their parents' income is 0.28 at 25--29 and 0.49 at 40--44, as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:headline}. A father's earnings bundle his hourly wage, the hours he works, the years he spends out of
work and the labor market he shares with other families, and each of these points to a different policy: schooling and
training if the wage is passed on, tax and transfer incentives if hours are, unemployment insurance if spells out of
work are, policy aimed at groups or places if the shared labor market is. This paper splits the IGE into these
components, by the son's age, and asks which of them carry its rise with age.

The parents' hourly wage carries a fifth of the IGE, their spells out of work a sixteenth, their hours and their place
almost nothing, and half of the IGE remains parental income at equal values of everything the data measure. Over
25--55 the parents' wage carries 19.0\%$^{***}$ of the IGE of 0.396$^{***}$, their hours 2.8\%$^{**}$, their spells
6.1\%$^{***}$ and the state's labor market 0.4\%. Among the parents' other resources, the father's schooling carries
12.9\%$^{***}$ and years on AFDC or TANF 6.6\%$^{**}$, while assets, transfer income and family size carry nothing; the rest
is 47.6\%$^{***}$. Measurement error hides part of the wage: measured on one half of the teen years and instrumented by
the other, its share rises from 28 to 37 percent. And where the PSID measures wealth, for sons born 1964 and later, the
parents' net worth carries 29.1\%$^{***}$ of the IGE and little rest remains. The split replicates where the data measure
the parents over several years: in the NLSY79 Children and Young Adults, whose mothers report every week of their work
history, the parents' spells carry 7.6\%$^{***}$. It fails where they are measured once: in the NLSY97 one year of the
parents' wages, hours and employment breaks carries nothing.

The IGE rises with age because what sons of richer parents bring to the labor market pays more as they age. In the
NLSY79 the earnings IGE rises from 0.340$^{***}$ at 25--29 to 0.506$^{***}$ at 50--54, and the son's AFQT, measured
before he enters the labor market, carries 62 percent of the rise; the parents' schooling carries most of the rest. In
the PSID the rise is within occupations and runs through schooling, whose return rises with age. Because the returns
to both schooling and measured skill rise with age, the pattern is that of skill complementing the human capital
accumulated on the job, not of employers learning about it \citep{altonji2001employer, lange2007speed}. It is age, not
the rise of the skill premium: in the NLSY97, whose sons reach the same ages twenty years later, the AFQT's contribution
rises with age by the same factor, and in the PSID the rise of the IGE holds within birth cohorts and with income-year
fixed effects.

The one component of what parents do whose share rises with age is their spells, and the reason is that a father's
layoff in his son's teens costs the son more as the son gets older. At equal parental income and equal values of every
other component, a layoff is unrelated to the son's earnings at 25--29 and lowers them by 0.110$^{***}$ log points per
layoff year at 45--49, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:layoff_age}. The cost is in the son's wage, it grows within the same son
($-0.236^{**}$ per ten years of age per unit share), and it follows layoffs during his childhood, not after he is 20:
among sons whose fathers were all laid off at some time, those whose father's layoff fell at 6--19 fall behind those
whose father's fell at 20--26. A plant closure, which displaces workers whatever their quality
\citep{gibbons1991layoffs}, carries no cost at any age, nor does a national decline of the father's industry, which
raises his unemployment. The costly layoffs are repeated ones with small income losses and short unemployment, in
fathers whose employment stays unstable after the son leaves home: unstable employment, not an income shock. Layoffs
in bad times, which say less about the father, cost about half as much. The cost holds in every birth cohort and is
larger when the job-end question is applied the same way in every wave. In the son's career it is a lower wage within his
occupation in his forties, not a worse first job and not his own job losses. The same contrast shows in which parental
income is passed on. Parental income predicted by the father's
layoffs passes on with an elasticity of 0.742$^{***}$, against 0.381$^{***}$ for the rest (difference 0.361$^{**}$);
income predicted by plant closures passes on with an elasticity of 0.074. Like \citet{shea2000does}, who finds that
income from sources beyond the parents' control matters little, the closures show that the lost money is not what is
passed on; the layoffs show that what comes with them is.

A father's childhood layoff pulls his sons and his daughters in opposite directions. In one specification for both, a
unit of teen layoff share changes the son's hourly wage between 25--29 and 45--49 by $-0.538^{**}$ and the daughter's by
$+0.387^{*}$: a difference of 0.925$^{***}$ ($p = 0.001$), and 0.254$^{**}$ a decade within the same person. The difference
holds when the job-end question is applied the same way in every wave and is positive in every birth cohort. The
daughters' gain is not selection into work or marriage: their employment does not change, and the gain survives holding
partnership and full-year work fixed. They also partner with men who earn more by 45--49. Whatever the layoff marks or
does, it is not a trait passed on to all of a father's children alike; it reaches sons and daughters differently.

The son's own spells run the other way. His years unemployed, displaced or without work carry 30.9\%$^{***}$ of the
earnings IGE at 25--29 and 8.7\%$^{***}$ at 45--49: the IGE of a young man is in large part about whether he works, that of
a man in mid-career about what he is paid. Net of his spells, the IGE at 25--29 is 0.47 of its value at 45--49 rather
than 0.62: the life-cycle bias \citep{haider2006life, nybom2016heterogeneous} is larger in what sons are paid than in
what they earn.

The components point to policy in three ways. Generous unemployment insurance in the son's teens goes with a smaller
spells component, 9.3\%$^{**}$ of the IGE against 13.3\%$^{***}$ where insurance is less generous, a difference in the
predicted direction that is not significant ($p = 0.50$); insurance simulated at the father's own wage does not weaken
the layoff's cost, as expected if the cost comes from unstable employment rather than from the income lost. The search friction of the family's group, measured by the job
finding of men of its race in its state, carries 8.2\%$^{***}$ of the IGE, all of it between races. And the components
that grow with age---the parents' wage, the father's schooling, the teen layoff---cannot be seen by evaluations of
childhood policies that score earnings around 30 \citep{hoynes2016long, jackson2016effects, bastian2018long,
chetty2016effects}. The EITC shows it: a family's maximum credit during the son's teens goes with lower earnings at
25--29 and none at 40--44, because the same sons' earnings grow faster with it (0.029$^{***}$ a decade per thousand
dollars).

The rise of the IGE with age is White, and the components say why. The White--Black gap in the IGE is $-0.04$ at 25--29
and 0.30 at 45--49; the elasticity of Black sons is flat in age. The father's schooling carries 0.065$^{***}$ of the White
and 0.002 of the Black IGE. The cost of a layoff to Black sons cannot be distinguished from the cost to White sons, but Black
fathers' layoffs are not concentrated in low-income families, so the rising cost raises the White IGE and not the Black
one: the racial difference in what is passed on through spells is in who is laid off, not in what a layoff does. For
Black sons family structure carries three fifths of the IGE and parental income at equal parents carries nothing.

\paragraph{Contributions and literature.} The paper contributes to three literatures. First, it splits the IGE on the
parents' side. Earlier accounts split it on the child's side, into the child's schooling, skills and attachment to work
\citep{blanden2007accounting, gregg2019intergenerational, rothstein2019inequality}, model the joint transmission of
wages and hours \citep{altonji2000intergenerational}, or ask whether parents' money matters
\citep{shea2000does, cardak2013intergenerational, black2005why}. Here the parents' wage, hours, spells and place are
components of one exact decomposition \citep{gelbach2016covariates}, measured by the son's age and replicated in four data
sets. Second, it adds the age of the son to the evidence on parental job loss, which estimates the effect of one shock
at one age \citep{oreopoulos2008intergenerational, hilger2016parental, kalil2011parental}: the cost of a layoff in the
son's teens appears after 40, and a plant closure carries none. Third, it connects the life-cycle bias of the IGE
\citep{haider2006life, grawe2006lifecycle, nybom2016heterogeneous, nybom2017biases, chetty2014where} to what is passed
on: the bias is the rising pay-off of skill and schooling, the delayed cost of a teen layoff and the fading weight of the
son's own spells. On race it dates the divergence of log persistence, known from the downward mobility of Black sons
of well-off parents \citep{hertz2005rags, bhattacharya2011nonparametric, mazumder2014black, chetty2020race}, to the
thirties, and locates it in the components.
