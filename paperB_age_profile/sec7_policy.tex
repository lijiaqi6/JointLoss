\section{Implications for Policy}\label{sec:policy}

The layoff result points to the stability of the father's employment, not to the income his layoffs cost, and the data
say that insurance as legislated does not reach it. Generous unemployment insurance in the son's teens goes with a
smaller stability component across states, but the difference is not significant, and insurance simulated at the
father's own wage, in its level or its duration, does not weaken the layoff's cost. The search friction faced by the
family's group---the job finding of men of its race in its state---carries a tenth of the IGE, entirely between races.
And the components that grow with the son's age are invisible to evaluations that score children's outcomes around 30;
the EITC, whose intent-to-treat exposure shows an age profile even where the credit barely reaches, is a caution about
such designs, and it is too small to move the IGE.

\subsection{What the layoff result asks of policy}

The cost of a father's childhood layoff follows repeated layoffs with small income losses and short unemployment, in
fathers whose employment stays unstable; it is not carried by the large income shocks, by parental income after the
son's teens, or by plant closures and industry declines; it does not come with the father's sector, occupation or union
job; and benefits that replace more of the lost income are not shown to weaken it. If the cost is what instability does
to the son, the margin is the stability of the father's employment---the churn of short jobs and repeated layoffs of
which unemployment insurance, with its waiting week and its rules for short and partial spells, replaces
little---and support for the son that reaches the years in which he finishes school and enters work. If it is what the
instability reveals about the father, the cost is a marker that tells policy which children to reach, not a lever. The
paper cannot choose between the two; both say that policies scored by the income they replace, and at the son's age
30, would miss it.

\subsection{Unemployment insurance in childhood}

If what a layoff passes on is in part the income and the turmoil that follow it, insurance that replaces more of the
lost earnings should weaken the stability component. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:ui_stability} measures the generosity of the
state's unemployment insurance as its maximum weekly benefit over its average weekly wage, averaged over the son's ages
12--18 in the state where he lived at 17, and splits sons at the median of generosity relative to their birth cohort.
The IGE is the same in both halves (0.374$^{***}$ and 0.373$^{***}$). The stability component is smaller where
insurance was more generous: 0.035$^{**}$ of the IGE (9.3\%$^{**}$) against 0.050$^{***}$ (13.3\%$^{***}$), and 0.071
against 0.101$^{**}$ at 45--49. The difference, $-4.1$ points of the IGE (s.e.\ 6.0, $p = 0.496$), is the predicted
direction and is not significant.\footnote{Sons born 1958 and later (3,495), whose teens fall in years covered by the
Department of Labor's statutory schedules. Code: \texttt{12b.UIGenerosity.py} (generosity) and
\texttt{82.UIStabilityChannel.py} (\texttt{output/ui\_stability\_channel.md}).}


The interactions in panel B of Appendix Table~\ref{tab:ui_stability} mostly point the same way. With state fixed effects, a standard deviation of generosity raises the
son's earnings by 0.034$^{**}$ ($p = 0.045$) per standard deviation of the father's closure share, and by 0.057$^{**}$
($p = 0.024$) at 40--55; the layoff interaction is positive and imprecise (0.021, $p = 0.19$); and generosity lowers the
son's share of years unemployed by 0.010$^{**}$ ($p = 0.037$) per standard deviation of the mother's unemployment. The
placebo---generosity interacted with the father's wage, which insurance should not offset---is zero ($-0.004$). The
signs are those of insurance offsetting the spells component, but each estimate alone is weak because generosity varies little
within a state and cohort (a standard deviation of 0.041, against 0.089 in all).\footnote{With the log of the maximum
total benefit instead of the replacement ratio the signs are the same and the estimates weaker (layoff $\times$ UI
0.024, $p = 0.12$). For Black sons the placebo on employment is 0.034$^{**}$ ($p = 0.017$).} 

A state's schedule is an imprecise measure of what a given family received, and the sharper measure does not support
insurance. Simulating the benefit each father's state law would have paid him---half his weekly earnings between the
state's minimum and maximum, in each year of the son's 12--18 from 1976---gives a replacement rate of 0.342 on average
that varies with the law and with where his wage sits relative to the cap. Within the same son, a standard deviation of
it changes the bend of the layoff's cost by $-0.038$ (s.e.\ 0.187), once the layoff's interaction with the father's
wage is held fixed; without that control the interaction is $-0.218^{*}$, the father's wage under another name, because
the cap makes the replacement rate fall with it (panel C of Table~\ref{tab:layoff_exog}).\footnote{Dependants'
allowances and the exact benefit formulas are not coded; the cap binds in three quarters of the window years. On the sons
whose teens fall in 1976 and later and whose father's wage is measured, the layoff's within-son cost is itself small
(0.040, s.e.\ 0.113); on all sons born 1976 and later it is $-0.547^{**}$ (Section~\ref{sec:stability}). Code:
\texttt{99.SimulatedReplacementRate.py}.} The duration of benefits gives the same answer. Weeks available in the
family's state---the regular maximum, Extended Benefits when the state's trigger was on, and the federal emergency
programmes of 1984--85, 1991--94, 2002--04 and 2008--13---average 34 weeks over the son's 12--18 (s.d.\ 10), and a
standard deviation more changes the bend of the layoff's cost by $-0.191$ (s.e.\ 0.395), with the layoff's interaction
with the state's unemployment rate held fixed, since the weeks are set by it.\footnote{Extended Benefits status from the
Labor Department's weekly ETA 539 report (1984 on); the federal programmes' tiers are approximated from their schedules
and the state's insured and total unemployment rates. 1,569 sons, 209 with a teen layoff year. Code:
\texttt{102.UIDurationWeeks.py}.} Unemployment insurance as legislated, in its level or its duration, is not shown to
weaken what a father's layoff passes on, and the designs are too imprecise to rule it out: the smallest effects they could
detect (80 percent power at 5 percent) are 17 points of the IGE for the split, against a spells share of 13 percent;
0.52 a decade per standard deviation of the simulated replacement rate, against a layoff cost of 0.24; and 1.1 for the
weeks. They exclude only insurance effects larger than the cost itself.

\subsection{Group search friction}

A family shares its labor market with other families of its race and place. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:group_race} measures the
search friction of the group as the monthly job finding of non-employed men of the son's race in his state during his
teens, from the CPS: 0.193 for White and 0.140 for Black men (difference $-0.054^{***}$). Added to the split of
Appendix~\ref{sec:passedon}, this measure carries 8.2\%$^{***}$ ($p = 0.004$) of the pooled IGE, against 1.9 percent for
the state's unemployment rate and labor-demand shocks on the same sons. Within race it carries nothing: 0.0 percent of
the White IGE and $-9.1$ percent (s.e.\ 14.5) of the Black.\footnote{Sons born 1976 and later (1,752), whose teens fall
in the CPS monthly files of 1994--2021; the job finding of women of the son's race enters the same component. Annual
re-employment of men of the son's race in the March CPS of 1977--92 carries nothing (0.006, $p = 0.37$). Code:
\texttt{17.AtlasFrictions.py} (measure) and \texttt{84.GroupFrictionByRace.py}
(\texttt{output/group\_friction\_by\_race.md}).} The group component is a contrast between races: Black families have
lower parental income and face lower job finding. Within a race, the friction of the family's place is not passed on.
In these data the group's job finding cannot be told apart from anything else that differs by race, so it says where
policy aimed at groups would act---job-search assistance, anti-discrimination enforcement---not that it would work.


\subsection{Transfers}

Years on AFDC or TANF carry 6.6\%$^{**}$ ($p = 0.015$) of the IGE at equal parental income and equal components, and the
share of parental income that came from transfers carries nothing ($-0.001$; Appendix~\ref{sec:passedon}). Receipt marks
the families whose sons do worse at equal income. The split cannot say whether receipt marks what brought the family
onto welfare or what welfare did, and holding total income fixed is not a test of what transfer dollars do: at equal
total income, a larger transfer share is lower earnings and a different family.

\subsection{The age of scoring}

The components that grow with the son's age are weighted lightly by evaluations that score outcomes early or pool them
across ages. Long-run evaluations of childhood policies differ in when they observe adults: some score earnings in the
twenties or early thirties---the EITC at 18--27 \citep{bastian2018long}, Moving to Opportunity \citep{chetty2016effects}
and the 31--37 window of \citet{chetty2020race}---while others pool adult outcomes over a wide range of ages, as the
evaluations of Food Stamps \citep{hoynes2016long} and school finance \citep{jackson2016effects} do; welfare calculations
often project lifetime earnings from early ages \citep{hendren2020unified}. The results here say that the age at which
an outcome is observed changes what a childhood shock or policy appears to do, so that estimates by age, rather than
early or pooled, show components that appear after 40. Between 25--29 and 45--49 the parents' wage component
rises from 0.035 to 0.097$^{***}$ (at 35--39), the father's schooling from 0.014 to 0.075$^{***}$, the spells component
from 0.017$^{**}$ to 0.060$^{***}$, and a teen layoff's cost from nothing to 0.110$^{***}$ a year. A policy that acts on
any of these---schooling, the stability of parents' employment, insurance in the son's teens---would be scored at 30
before most of its effect has appeared. The son's own spells run the other way: a policy that raises young men's
employment shows its effect on the IGE early, and its share falls with age.

The EITC is a caution rather than an example. Following \citet{bastian2018long}, a son's exposure is the maximum
federal and state credit his family could receive, given its number of children, in each year of his 13--18; the
variation comes from the federal schedule by year and number of children, which expanded most in 1993--96, and from the
state credits. Scored at 25--29, a thousand dollars more of exposure goes with earnings lower by 0.016$^{*}$
($p = 0.052$), and by 0.016$^{**}$ with log parental income held fixed (the credit is not in PSID family income); scored
at 40--44, the coefficient is $-0.003$; and within the same son exposure goes with faster earnings growth,
0.029$^{***}$ ($p = 0.002$) a decade (Table~\ref{tab:eitc_age}). Read alone, this is a credit that looks costly at 25
and pays off later. It is not the credit. The same profile appears where the credit barely reaches: within son,
exposure goes with faster growth by 0.020$^{**}$ in the bottom third of parental income, where 55 percent of window
years have earned income in the credit's range, and by 0.032$^{***}$ ($p<0.001$) in the top third, where 3 percent do
and the credit the family would receive averages \$33 a year; the lower earnings at 25--29 are there too
($-0.024^{***}$ in the bottom third, $-0.013$ in the top). Intent-to-treat exposure varies with the number of children
and the birth year, and the sons of larger families in later cohorts follow different earnings profiles for reasons of
their own; exposure measured the same way at the son's 6--11 or 19--24 is too closely correlated with the teen measure
(0.81 and 0.88) to separate the two. What the example shows is how a schedule-based design scored at one age can
suggest an effect of either sign that a check where the policy does not bind would not support.\footnote{Exposure is
intent to treat: it depends only on the year, the state and the number of children, with birth-year, state and
number-of-children fixed effects. Thirds of log parental income within birth year; earned income in the credit's range
and the credit the family would receive at its actual earnings are imputed from the schedule. In the third of families
most likely to be eligible, predicted from characteristics fixed before the son's teens, the growth is
0.064$^{***}$ a decade, against 0.017 in the others; the 1993--96 expansion for families with two or more children
gives $-0.079$ at 25--29 and $+0.046$ at 40--44, neither significant; daughters' earnings at 25--29 are higher by
0.028$^{***}$, subject to the same caution. Code: \texttt{101.EITCByAge.py}, \texttt{126.EITCDesign.py},
\texttt{129.EITCChecks.py} (\texttt{output/eitc\_checks.md}).}

The credit is also too small to move the IGE, and the data find no change in it. It reaches these families as income,
not through more work by their mothers on average, because married and single mothers respond in opposite directions,
as in \citet{eissa2004taxes} and \citet{eissa1996labor}: a thousand dollars more of maximum credit in a window year
raises the credit the family would receive at its actual earnings by \$116$^{***}$ for married and \$498$^{***}$ for
single mothers, lowers married mothers' employment by 0.032$^{**}$ ($p = 0.028$) and their hours by 71$^{**}$ a year,
and raises single mothers' employment by 0.016 ($p = 0.48$) and their hours by 51 ($p = 0.33$); pooled, the two cancel
($-0.009$, $p = 0.44$). Most of these mothers already work (81 percent in the sons' window years), and those who do not
are seldom looking: 9 percent of married and 37 percent of single non-working mothers, unchanged by the credit. Asked
why they are not looking, mothers of children under 18 name family responsibilities and child care (49 percent) and
disability (16 percent), and hardly ever the labor market (3 percent); in 1971, most non-working wives were said to be
able to find a job easily (60 percent). Even if the credit passed on to sons like other parental income, with the whole
IGE as the pass-through, it would lower the IGE by 0.007 at 25--29 and 0.011 at 45--49, because it is a few hundred
dollars a year in the bottom third of families (\$700, against family income of \$24,000). The data detect only
changes several times larger, about 0.08 at 25--29 and 0.14 at 40--44 per standard deviation of exposure, and they find
none: the interaction of exposure with log parental income is $-0.0001$ ($p = 0.98$) at 25--29 and $-0.0044$
($p = 0.54$) at 40--44, and exposure does not change the rank--rank slope at any age.\footnote{Mothers = the wife or
female head of the son's family unit in his window years 1979 on; single = female head. The credit is imputed from the
family's head and wife labor income, its number of children and the year's schedule (joint filers when married), times
one plus the state rate; the PSID does not record receipt. Looking = `unemployed, looking for work' among mothers not
working at the interview. Reasons: `main reason not looking', asked from 2019 of heads and spouses not working and not
looking, women with a child under 18 in all PSID families (1,554); 1971: the head's answers about a non-working wife
under 65 (782); in 1976 and 1981--83, 13 to 26 percent of wives out of the labor force who were thinking of a job had
done anything in the last four weeks to find one. Calibration: the son's gain is the IGE times
$\ln(1 + \text{credit}/\text{family income})$, whose slope on log parental income is $-0.026$. Code:
\texttt{127.EITCMothers.py}, \texttt{128.EITCAndIGE.py}, \texttt{129.EITCChecks.py}.}

\subsection{By race}

Two conditions at the start of the son's career have been proposed for the racial gap in persistence: the labor
market he enters and public employment, where Black sons start more often. Neither creates the racial opening of the
IGE, which is 0.208$^{**}$ among sons who entered tight markets and 0.213$^{***}$ among sons who started in the private
sector (Appendix~\ref{app:entry}). Generous insurance offsets the father's closures most clearly for White sons at
40--55 (0.062$^{**}$, $p = 0.019$) and the mother's unemployment most clearly for Black sons ($-0.020^{**}$ on the son's
years unemployed, $p = 0.011$).
