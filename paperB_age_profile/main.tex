\title{Fathers' Layoffs, Daughters and the IGE
\thanks{\footnotesize Preliminary draft; working title. Each number in the text comes from the table or the code named
next to it (EVIDENCE.md lists every estimate with its script); the tables are generated by \texttt{28.PaperBTables.py}, \texttt{44.PaperBReviewTables.py},
\texttt{90.PaperBRaceNeutralTables.py} and \texttt{100.PaperBFableTables.py}.}}
\author{Jiaqi Li \\ University of Sheffield}
\date{\today}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent The intergenerational elasticity of earnings (IGE) rises with the age at which sons are observed, from 0.28
at 25--29 to 0.47 at 40--44 in the PSID, and one thing parents do goes with a cost that rises sharply with the son's age: the
father's layoffs during the son's childhood, a small part of the IGE itself. At equal parental income and equal values of the parents' wages, hours,
other spells and place, a father's layoff in the son's teens is unrelated to the son's earnings at 25--29 and lowers
them by 11 log points per layoff year at 45--49---in the hourly wage across ages and in earnings within the same son,
negative in every birth cohort, under one
rule for recording job ends, and more than a layoff after the son is 20. Plant closures and national declines of the
father's industry carry no detectable cost; the cost is largest after repeated layoffs with small income losses, in fathers whose
employment stays unstable; layoffs in bad times cost the son about half as much, though they cost the father at least as much earnings and no work, so the son's cost follows the father's employment, not his income loss (none of these differences is significant); and the cost is a lower wage within the son's
occupation in his forties, not a worse first job. Sons of laid-off fathers are more often self-employed, at 25--29 and
more so at 45--49, and self-employment pays them less with age: their reported earnings and hourly wage fall behind in their
self-employed years and not in their years as employees. The same layoffs go with the opposite for daughters: their wages rise with
age, a difference from sons of 93 log points per unit share between 25--29 and 45--49 ($p = 0.001$), not explained by
selection into work, marriage or observation. Around these results, the parents' wage and, where it is measured,
their wealth carry between two fifths and a half of the IGE. The wealth is housing: the home and other real estate carry 21
percent of the IGE and business equity 2 percent. And wealth has its own age profile: the rank--rank slope of sons'
net worth on their parents' keeps rising into the late thirties, from 0.23 to 0.41, after earnings ranks have stopped
rising, and not through gifts or inheritances. The son's measured skill carries three fifths of the earnings IGE's rise
with age in the NLSY79 and a quarter in the NLSY97. Unemployment
insurance at the father's wage is not shown to weaken the sons' cost, and the EITC is too small to move the IGE detectably.
\end{abstract}
