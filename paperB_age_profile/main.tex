\title{Built in the First Decade of the Career: The Age Profile of\\ Intergenerational Persistence and the
Black--White Gap
\thanks{\footnotesize Preliminary draft. Each number in the text comes from the table or the code named next to it; the
tables are generated by \texttt{IGE/28.PaperBTables.py} and \texttt{IGE/44.PaperBReviewTables.py}.}}
\author{Jiaqi Li \\ University of Sheffield}
\date{\today}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent How persistent incomes are across generations depends on when children are observed. In the PSID the
elasticity of sons' earnings with respect to parental income rises from 0.28 at ages 25--29 to 0.49 at 40--44, and the
rise is concentrated among White sons: the White--Black gap is zero in the first decade of the career and 0.29 at
40--49, three times its value at 31--37. With one year of parental income the gap opens by 0.09 log points in four
public data sets, 0.19 corrected for measurement error, while the rank--rank slopes of sons' family income do not diverge. The divergence is
in wages and is built at 25--34: in the job a son holds then, to which access differs by race at equal parental income,
and in a return to schooling that rises with age much faster for White than for Black sons, also within the same job.
Recessions at entry and public employment do not create the gap. A life-cycle model that nests four explanations
selects one: parental income raises the growth of human capital for White sons and not for Black sons (a channel seen
with a multi-year measure of parental income and absorbed by schooling with a one-year measure, in the PSID as in the
NLSY79 and NLSY97), and schooling buys Black sons a higher wage at entry and slower growth; a racial difference in
access to good jobs is rejected. The first channel accounts for three fifths of the gap at 45--49 and two fifths of its
opening since 25--29, and equalising schooling, access or entry conditions
closes less than a tenth of it.
\end{abstract}
