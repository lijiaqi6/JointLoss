\title{Fathers' Layoffs, Daughters and the IGE
\thanks{\footnotesize Preliminary draft; working title. Each number in the text comes from the table or the code named
next to it; the tables are generated by \texttt{28.PaperBTables.py}, \texttt{44.PaperBReviewTables.py},
\texttt{90.PaperBRaceNeutralTables.py} and \texttt{100.PaperBFableTables.py}.}}
\author{Jiaqi Li \\ University of Sheffield}
\date{\today}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent The intergenerational elasticity of earnings (IGE) rises with the age at which sons are observed, from 0.28
at 25--29 to 0.47 at 40--44 in the PSID, and one thing parents do carries a share of it that rises with age: the
father's layoffs during the son's childhood. At equal parental income and equal values of the parents' wages, hours,
other spells and place, a father's layoff in the son's teens is unrelated to the son's earnings at 25--29 and lowers
them by 11 log points per layoff year at 45--49---in the wage, within the same son, in every birth cohort, under one
rule for recording job ends, and more than a layoff after the son is 20. Plant closures and national declines of the
father's industry carry no detectable cost; the costly layoffs are repeated ones with small income losses, in fathers whose
employment stays unstable; layoffs in bad times cost about half as much; and the cost is a lower wage within the son's
occupation in his forties, not a worse first job. Sons of laid-off fathers are more often self-employed, at 25--29 and
more so at 45--49, and self-employment pays them less with age: their earnings and hourly wage fall behind in their
self-employed years and not in their years as employees. The same layoffs pull daughters the other way: their wages rise with
age, a difference from sons of 0.93 log points per unit share between 25--29 and 45--49 ($p = 0.001$), not explained by
selection into work, marriage or observation. Around these results, the parents' wage and, where it is measured,
their wealth carry about half of the IGE, and the son's skill at entry most of its rise with age. Unemployment
insurance at the father's wage is not shown to weaken the sons' cost, and the EITC illustrates how the age at which a
childhood policy is scored can change its estimated effect.
\end{abstract}
