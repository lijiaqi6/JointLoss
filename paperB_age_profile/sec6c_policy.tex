\section{Shocks, Policies and the Age of Scoring}\label{sec:policy}

The fact and its mechanism bear on how public policies and economic shocks affect racial disparities in two ways. The
first needs no new design: a policy or shock whose effects on the racial gap are measured before the mid-thirties is
scored before the gap has opened. Long-run evaluations of childhood policies measure adult earnings in the late twenties
or early thirties---Food Stamps \citep{hoynes2016long}, school finance \citep{jackson2016effects}, the EITC
\citep{bastian2018long}, Moving to Opportunity \citep{chetty2016effects}, the 31--37 window of \citet{chetty2020race},
and the welfare calculations that project lifetime earnings from those ages \citep{hendren2020unified}. If a policy
raises the human capital level of Black children but leaves the pay-off of their schooling and their access to good
jobs unchanged, the age profile of Section~\ref{sec:ageprofile} implies that its effect on the racial gap measured at 30
overstates its effect at 45. The second way is through the mechanism of Sections~\ref{sec:careers}--\ref{sec:fathers}:
shocks and policies that act on the first decade of the career, on access to good jobs and on the pay-off of schooling.
This section tests three, each chosen because the mechanism makes a prediction for it and the public data can carry it.
Shocks to the household a son grows up in---his parents' job losses and the safety net that insures the family---are the
subject of a companion paper; the shocks here hit the son's own career.\footnote{The one object the two papers share is
the father's displacement re-scored by the son's age, reported below. Code for this section:
\texttt{IGE/49.PaperBPolicyShocks.py} (\texttt{output/paperB\_policy\_shocks.md}).}

\paragraph{Labor-market conditions at entry.} A recession at labor-market entry lowers earnings for years
\citep{kahn2010long, oreopoulos2012short}, more so for nonwhite and less-educated workers
\citep{schwandt2019unlucky}. In the model it would lower the chance that a son's first offer is a good job. I date entry
at 18 plus years of schooling beyond 12 and measure the unemployment rate at entry in the state where the son's parents
lived when he was 17, with state and entry-year fixed effects (Table~\ref{tab:entry}). Entry conditions do not move
access to good starting jobs on average: a percentage point of state unemployment at entry changes the probability of
holding a well-paid, fast-growing job at 25--34 by 0.004 (0.008), and the mean rate over the first ten years by 0.004
(0.010). They do steepen access in parental income for White sons: after a slack entry, the access of White sons rises
with parental income by a further 0.009$^{**}$ (0.004) per point of unemployment and log point of parental income, and
not for Black sons ($-0.001$, s.e.\ 0.005). Wages carry the scar. Nationally, a point of unemployment at entry lowers
sons' wages at 25--34 by 0.013$^{**}$ (0.005); in the state design, Black sons' wages at 35--55 are lower by
0.035$^{*}$ (0.020) and White sons' are not (0.007, s.e.\ 0.016; difference 0.042$^{*}$, $p=0.096$). Sons who entered in
markets slacker than their state and year norm show a larger racial opening of the earnings IGE than sons who entered in
tight markets (0.362$^{***}$ against 0.208$^{**}$; difference 0.154, s.e.\ 0.119; Table~\ref{tab:openinggroups}): the
point estimate says that a slack entry adds about 0.15 to the opening, but the difference is not significant.
Entry conditions therefore do not create the gap, which opens by 0.208$^{**}$ among sons who entered tight markets, and they
do not move the average access of a cohort; they widen the parental-income gradient of White sons' access and leave
a longer wage scar on Black sons. For the model, the first stage on access is a precise zero, so the model gets no
entry shifter of access; the entry-conditions estimates serve as validation.

\paragraph{The public sector.} Public employment has been a main route of Black men into stable, well-paid jobs, with
pay scales that compress later growth. If Black sons' high return to schooling at 25--29 and its flat profile came
from the public sector, the racial difference in the pay-off of schooling would be a feature of sectors, not of
accumulation. Black sons are more likely to start in government: 19.1 percent of Black and 12.4 percent of White sons
spend most of 25--34 in federal, state or local government, and at equal parental income White sons are 5.9 points less
likely to ($-0.059^{**}$, s.e.\ 0.025). Government starters are also more often in good jobs (17
percent of Black and 42 percent of White government starters, against 3 and 13 percent of private starters). But the
sector does not carry the divergence. Among sons who start in the private sector, the racial opening of the earnings IGE
is 0.213$^{***}$ (0.058), against 0.232$^{***}$ for all sons (difference $-0.019$, s.e.\ 0.035; Table~\ref{tab:openinggroups}),
access to good jobs differs by race at equal parental income by 0.048$^{***}$ (0.014), and within the private sector the
return to schooling rises with age by 0.058$^{***}$ for White and 0.027$^{**}$ for Black sons, a difference of
0.032$^{**}$ (0.013), the same as in all sectors. Public employment is a policy fact about where Black sons start, not
the source of the racial difference in the pay-off of schooling, and the model does not need a third job type.

\paragraph{A childhood shock re-scored by the son's age.} The age-of-scoring argument can be put to one shock directly.
A father figure's displacement at the son's 0--17 is associated with lower earnings of the son at 25--34 (by 0.079$^{***}$,
s.e.\ 0.029, against sons never exposed with the same pre-event family income, parental schooling and birth year) and
with a coefficient of $-0.053$ (0.043) at 35--44; the change between the two ages is 0.026 (0.037), and it does not
differ significantly by race (White 0.047, Black $-0.019$; difference 0.066, s.e.\ 0.090; Table~\ref{tab:fatherdispage}).
For family income the association is $-0.065^{**}$ at 25--34 and $-0.090^{**}$ at 35--44. This shock's measured effect
does not change with the age at which it is scored: it acts on the level of the son's earnings, the model's initial
human capital level, and not on their growth. The age of scoring matters for policies and shocks that act on the
pay-off of schooling and on access to good jobs; it matters less for those, like this one, that act on levels.
