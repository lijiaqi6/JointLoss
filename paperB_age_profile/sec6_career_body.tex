
The cost is a lower wage within the son's occupation in mid-career; it is not where he starts and not his own
instability. Splitting his log wage into the CPS wage score of his occupation and his wage within it, the layoff's change
of $-0.603^{***}$ between 25--29 and 45--49 is $-0.392^{**}$ within the occupation and $-0.171^{**}$ between occupations
(panel C of Table~\ref{tab:layoff_mechanism}). Appendix~\ref{sec:skill} finds that the job a son holds at 25--34 predicts
how far he pulls away; the layoff does not work through it. A teen layoff lowers the chance of starting in a well-paid,
fast-growing job by only 0.041 ($p = 0.15$), and holding the son's early industry and occupation group fixed leaves the
within-son cost at $-0.225^{**}$ (against $-0.240^{**}$). Nor does the son repeat his father's instability: his own
displacement, unemployment, job changes and same-employer wage growth do not move with his father's teen layoffs. The one
trace is tenure, shorter by 4.2 years per unit share at 45--49 ($p = 0.093$), about seven months for one layoff
year.\footnote{Code: \texttt{106.LayoffCareerMechanism.py}.} Schooling, whose return rises with age
(Section~\ref{sec:ageprofile}), carries a sixth of the cost: a teen layoff barely changes a son's schooling ($-0.19$
years, s.e.\ 0.37), and holding schooling and its rising return fixed moves the within-son slope from $-0.236^{**}$ to
$-0.190^{**}$ and the change between 25--29 and 45--49 from $-0.914^{***}$ to $-0.763^{***}$.\footnote{Code:
\texttt{115.LayoffSchooling.py}.} Neither the son's health nor his place carries it: a father's teen layoff does not
change his son's chance of reporting fair or poor health or a work-limiting condition, whether he still lives in the
state he grew up in, or that state's unemployment rate, and holding any of them fixed leaves the within-son cost
unchanged ($-0.270^{**}$ against $-0.268^{**}$ with health held fixed).\footnote{Self-rated health of the son as head
from 1984, work limitation 1994--2015. Code: \texttt{116.SonsHealthPlace.py}.}

The cost is concentrated in the sons' self-employment. Sons of laid-off fathers are more often self-employed, by
0.097$^{**}$ ($p = 0.048$) at 25--29 and 0.271$^{**}$ ($p = 0.026$) at 45--49 per unit share, and when they are, their
earnings fall behind with age: within the same son the slope is $-0.885^{***}$ ($p<0.001$) in his self-employed years
and $-0.084$ ($p = 0.41$) in his years as an employee, and the interaction of the layoff, age and self-employment is
$-0.427^{**}$ ($p = 0.015$); the hourly wage shows the same contrast ($-0.609^{**}$ against $-0.004$). They are not
following self-employed fathers. Sons of self-employed fathers are twice as often self-employed (0.316 against 0.145 at
45--55), but without them a father's layoff raises his son's self-employment as much (0.118$^{**}$ at 25--29,
0.314$^{**}$ at 45--49), and the cost in the son's self-employed years is $-1.024^{***}$ ($p<0.001$) a decade, against
$-0.174^{*}$ ($p = 0.069$) in his years as an employee (\texttt{119.FatherSelfEmployment.py}). Nor is it business
income moved out of earnings: an owner splits his business income into a labor part, which is in his earnings, and an
asset part, which is not, and adding the asset part leaves the cost in his self-employed years at $-0.812^{***}$
($p = 0.002$). The cost is not in the business income at all---the labor part of it does not move with the father's
layoffs (0.079, s.e.\ 0.468)---but in the wages and salaries of these sons, many of whom work for themselves and for
others or pay themselves a salary: the interaction of the layoff, age and self-employment is $-0.804^{**}$
($p = 0.023$) on that part of their income, and their hours do not move.\footnote{Labor and asset parts of the head's
business income, 1976--2023; in self-employed years the labor part is 25--31 percent of earnings, and the asset part is
positive in a third of them. Code: \texttt{123.SonsBusinessIncome.py} (\texttt{output/sons\_business\_income.md}).} Union coverage and
government employment do not move with the father's layoffs in a way that carries the cost, and holding the size of
the son's workplace fixed leaves it unchanged.\footnote{Self-employed = works for himself, alone or also for others;
4,386 self-employed son-years of 1,317 sons. Self-employment is chosen and its income is measured with more noise, so the
contrast locates the cost rather than explaining it. Code: \texttt{118.SonsEmployerType.py}.} What lowers the pay of these sons within their
occupation in their forties---skills acquired after school, effort, or the jobs within an occupation they hold---the
data here do not separate.
